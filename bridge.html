CIV 102 Bridge Project Design Summary

The Trumphant Trapezoid

The Opportunity

The opportunity is to design a matboard bridge that allows for a toy train to go accross.

Requirements include: the bridge must bridge the gap between two supports, and be able a certain height.
As well, the bridge can only be constructed from limited matboard and glue, so we need to make good use of our limited material. Matboard is essentially paper, so its strength is limited as well.
Evluation criteria include: the maximum train weight the bridge can hold before it fails, and the self-weight of the bridge.

Using CIV102 course knowledge and code, we would need to come up with a design that fulfills the requirements, and optimizes for evluation criteria.

CIV102 Terminologies

Directional terms refer to the bridge placed left to right, in the direction of train travel.

  1. Deck: the top horizontal surface of the bridge.
  2. Cross section: the side-view of the bridge; key for structural design.
  3. Diaphragm / Stiffners: internal plates that increase stiffness and reduce buckling.
  4. Centroidal axis: the "center of mass" of the cross section; needs to be optimized to the right height.
  5. Flexural compression and Shear: the two types of failure-related actions that we consider in our analysis.
  6. Splice Connection: the matboard is not long enough to cover the whole span, so we need to connect the two separate pieces of the bridge together. This connection is what we call the splice connection.

Design Report Summary

Through several well-informed iterations, we converged on a trapezoidal cross section that had a factor of safety of 2.53, as predicted by engineering beam theory and thin plate buckling theory. Our bridge is 1265 mm wide. Our bridge, the Trumphant Trapezoid, has a trapezoidal cross section, allowing the centroidal axis to be closer to the top deck, strengthening the bridge against flexural compression given the increased material strength in tension which resides on the bottom. We have two and a half layers of top deck, for the same purpose. Our bridge has 10 unevenly-spaced diaphragms, to efficiently protect the bridge against shear buckling which is highest at the supports.

Our Process

Since the opportunity is somewhat well-defined by the course instructors, the framing/diverging/converging process is simplified, compared to Praxis projects. Without additional framing, we began a linear diverging and converging process. We iterated on our bridge cross-section 6 major times, and then planned out the matboard layout, diaphragms, and construction details. Then, we began constructing the bridge (which is also a prototype).

What These Projects Taught Me About Complexity and Simplicity

The bridge project helped me gain a deeper understanding of the Hoover Dam model: real life is complex, so any design we do must simplify real life. This realization came from the fact that our bridge was predicted to hold 868 Newtons, but in reality, our splice connection gave out at around 250 Newtons. In our words, our analysis simplfied the splice connection, but this simplification caused the implementation to fail. To connect to the Hoover Dam model: the implementation (building, prototyping) is what connects theory (mathematical models) and real life designs.

The lesson I learned was that in our design process, we must keep the complexity of the original opportunity in mind. For example, the Quebec bridge [3] fell down not because the calculations were wrong, but because the designer failed to account for the self-weight of the bridge. We can correct our simplifications in three ways:

  • We develop better models for reality. For example, for our bridge project, we could develop a better model for the splice connection. This wasn't a viable option for us, due to our limited knowledge.
  • We use engineering experience. Professor Bentz talks about "experience" as the third rule of engineering: "To find the answer, you must already know the answer" [4] Engineers nowadays would be able to correct this mistake because they have more experience in building bridges.
  • We use prototypes to test implementation. If our bridge project led to an actual bridge instead of a matboard bridge, the splice connection failure of our matboard bridge would show that something was inefficient with our theoretical analysis.

CTMFs Used

Iterations in Diverging/Converging [Diverging/Converging]

Iteration: a "round" of diverging / converging. In this project, it meant that we came up with some new designs, picked one, and verified its performance.

Having many iterations of diverging helps us combine many simple design decisions into one final working design. Since the framing of the opportunity was already defined by course instructors, the design space is more constrained than Praxis-styled open-ended design opportunities, so we didn't need to diverge to generate many design candidates. Instead, we worked in a linear diverging and converging process, where we did calculations to find the weakest structures of the bridge's cross section, and came up with a better design by improving the weakest structures. This linear approach felt much more organized and methodological than the Praxis workflow. Each iteration was relatively simple and consistent, allowing us to build up to the final, more complicated design with smaller, simpler steps. However, this is only possible because the design space is not large. In the future, I will choose the design process based on scope: broader diverging (more breadth) in early ideation, and a more linear process (more depth) when I need a working solution quickly.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: A more complicated design process with more breadth can be more suited for open-ended opportunities, while a simpler, depth-focused design process can be more suited for more time-constrained, scoped-down opportunities. The complexity and shape of the design process should be appropriate for the framing of the design.

Reference Designs [Diverging]

Reference design: A design that we find via secondary research, that can provide useful information for our design opportunity.

A reference design helped us develop a unique bridge; however, whether or not the reference design works in practice requires extra verification. When we looked at bridge designs online, we noticed that highway bridges often use a trapezoidal shape to raise the centroidal axis, which helps the bridge resist flexural compression. However, this more creative design made bridge construction harder, since the tilt in the bridge made it hard to press in the glue. From this, I learned that reference designs can be useful, but we also need to consider how the reference design would work in our specific situation. In our case, our team valued getting a strong design that demonstrated our structural design knowledge, but if we were designing for a bridge that would work in practice, we would have focused more on Design for Construction.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Our trapezoidal design was more complex, but this made constrution difficult. This shows that reference designs drawn from secondary research require verification and validation against the specific opportunity that we are working on.

Analysis (Verification Method) [Converging]

Analysis as a verification method: Using mathematical and physical calculations to evalute how a design would performance with a metric.

Through the disconnect between our theoretical and actual matboard bridge performance, I learned that analysis as a verification method is limited. We originally predicted the bridge to fail at 1143 Newtons, which we lowered to 868 Newtons after account for the limitations of our construction. But even with this adjustment, our analysis failed to account for the splice connection, causing unexpected early failure. For an actual bridge design, this would be catastrophic: designs that are going to be implemented in reality should always go through more rigourous prototyping and different modes of proxy testing to ensure safety. Using rigourous verification is part of the due diligence we would have to do as professional engineers.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: This case showed the tradeoff between simplicity and complexity in verification. Verification that only included analysis fails to consider a critical failure mode. The large gap between predicted and actual failure load demonstrates why real designs need layered verification, not analysis alone.
