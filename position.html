Introduction to Position:

Currently, I hold the view that engineers should be able to perceive an opportunity holistically and embrace its complexity, while still being able to simplify the opportunity to represent effectively and build useful things.

In my position statement at the start of Praxis II, I argued that engineers need to embrace complexity, since my Praxis I project and bridge project both failed to perceive the full complexity of the opportunity , causing the implementation of the design to fail. I argued that this inconsistency between theory and reality can be bridged through prototyping and experience. However, in Praxis II, our project aimed too much to address every aspect of the opportunity, which led to us not being able to fully explore the specific metrics we would need in our RFP. This led me to my current view that a balance between complexity and simplicity is necessary.

Now, I will present an annotated version of the position statement I wrote at the beginning of Praxis II.

Position Statement: Moving Towards Embracing Complexity

Introduction

MY150 was so quiet, you could hear a needle drop. My footsteps echoed as I walked up the stairs, as the lights above filled the lecture hall with a fuzzy, dream-like golden light. Strange – since when was MY150 this empty? I’m not sure why I was here – or perhaps I had simply forgotten the purpose. 

But then, I saw a little girl, napping with her arms folded on top of a table. Laid out before her was a math exercise book, written in Chinese. Upon hearing my footsteps, she woke up, still groggy. 

“Hello, big sister,” she said. “Or should I say – me of the future? I am you, when you were 10 years old.”

“Oh.” Unsure of what else I could do, I sat down across from her. “So, you’re in 4th grade? In China?”

“Yep!” She chirped. “You?”

“I’m in engineering,” I replied. The girl in front of me claims to be in 4th grade, but she knew perfect English, which I didn’t at the time. Somehow, I didn’t find that odd.

“Oh cool!” The girl giggled. “Am I going to be an engineer when I grow up?” 

“Looks like it.” I shrugged. 

 

My original position statement was a story, because stories often contain more nuance and complexity than plain technical writing; however, I realize now that my representation may have obscured the point I was trying to get accross. I used a dialogue between my younger self and myself of the present to represent a more nuanced introspection of my position. However, the feedback I got for my original position statement was that it took way longer than 5 minutes to read, causing my position to not come accross effectively. So maybe, a more concise representation would have been more appropriate for the assignment. In other words, sometimes, complex representations obscure meaning; depending on the context, concise representations may be more useful.

 

What I view as Engineering Design: Applying Theory in Practice

 “So, what do engineers even do?”

“We engage in engineering design.” As soon as the words left my mouth, I knew that that was not going to be an answer that satisfied her. Ten-year-old me had no idea that she would study engineering in the future, so she is going to have so many questions…

“So you… make stuff?” The girl asked.

“Not quite. Let me rephrase – I think engineering design is a process in which scientific knowledge and principles are applied to produce a useful product. It’s the intersection of applied science and design. You see– “ I sketched in the margins of her notebook:

“For me, design is a process that turns an abstract opportunity into something tangible that has utility. Good design is often the product of a good process that usually consists of framing, diverging, and converging. If you knit a scarf for your mom to keep her warm, maybe you are already doing design!”

“Alright, but engineers don’t just knit scarfs! They’re way cooler!”

“Yes, because they are also applied scientists. Engineers apply scientific knowledge (math, science…) and scientific methods (experimentation, benchmarking…) to make something useful. Without science, I don’t think engineers would be quite as cool.”

“Okay. ” Me-of-the-past looked confused. “I didn’t understand everything you just said, but I think I get the idea…?”

“That’s how I feel in class every day…” I sighed. “It’s normal that we don’t understand things when they get complicated; engineering students feel the same. But I think that, sometimes, complicated-ness – or, complexity – really isn’t such a bad thing.”

“How can ‘complicated’ be a good thing? Big sister, would you rather get complicated questions or simpler questions on your exams?”

“Simp – Well…” I cleared my throat. “You see, it’s different…”

My definition for engineering design has not changed much: I still believes that it is applying science to make something useful. Connecting this back to the Hoover Dam Model: engineering design attempts to bridge the gap between reality (technology) and theory (science, math...), and in that process, makes something useful.

 

My Experiences and Beliefs: Recognizing and Embracing Complexity

 

“Complexity always exists in the design process, so embracing it is very important. Specifically, the engineering opportunity is often not well-defined, so the framing of the opportunity should take uncertainty into account; additionally, team members often hold diverse viewpoints, so we would need to form our own opinions but hold them lightly.”

I realized that, for some reason, I was showing off my praxis knowledge in front of her. This led the girl to look at me with a blank stare. “So you’re saying that the real world is complicated, so we just have to learn to deal with it?”

“Yeah, something like that. When things are so complicated, they become uncertain. ” I replied. “And that’s not what your math teachers want. You remember the questions that the teacher made you do, where they ask you ‘there are 4 red balls and 6 green balls in a bag, what are the chances you take out 2 reds and 1 green?’”

“Right! I hate those questions! Who’s going to care about red balls and green balls?”

“Exactly. The red and green balls are a simplification. Real-life doesn’t work like that – things that have to be implemented in real life are always more complicated, and they can’t be described by a few lines on a paper. Another example would be – last year, in a structures class, we had to build a matboard bridge.”

“Oh cool!” The girl’s eyes gleamed. “Bridges!”

“We did a bunch of calculations, but because we forgot to account for a matboard connection, our bridge failed miserably. There was only one small train on it before it went, ‘crack!’” 

“Oh.” The girl’s shoulders slumped. “That’s sad.”

“Yeah… Well, I learned a lesson from it, and that is that no matter how good our calculations are, real-life implementation is always more uncertain, and therefore, more complicated – therefore, we need testing, experience, and an iterative design process. Even though we had a semester’s worth of knowledge and a 30-page document to guide our analysis, we still forgot to account for the matboard connection. So – thank goodness it wasn’t an actual bridge. Knowledge isn’t enough. Because of the complexity of the real world, only repeated prototyping, rigorous testing, and engineering experience could hold up an actual bridge.”

I continued: “Additionally, in design, the opportunity is not quite as simple.  It’s often something similar to a ‘wicked problem’.”

“Wicked? Scary…”

“Yeah, that’s how I feel about my praxis designs sometimes…” I shook my head. “However, ‘wicked’ just means that the opportunity itself cannot be clearly defined – there is no stopping point, nor are there right or wrong solutions. 

“Therefore,” I went on, “sometimes, in the design process, we have to go back to redefine the opportunity. Framing, diverging and converging is not a linear process; instead, they should be used iteratively when they are needed. Like in our praxis project: we were trying to make a soda can opener, but our initial analysis of the soda can opening mechanism was wrong, so we often had to go back and learn more about the soda can when we were trying to come up with solutions.”

“Sounds like hard work… wait, what’s praxis?”

“You’ll see.” I smiled at my ten-year-old self. “But yeah, the hard work definitely became easier because I had a great team.” 

“So, I guess you guys got along well?”

“Well… not all the time.” I scratched my chin.

“But you still had fun?”

“Yeah! Sometimes, working with people who are different from you can produce great work. In other words: accepting the diverse viewpoints of my teammates and embracing the complexity of our opinions helped me reach commitment. In praxis, we talk about the Perry Model, where we strive to have our own opinions that join our own values, and analysis of other’s opinions; however, we hold these opinions lightly, since we should be willing to change if better information appears.” 

“So… no one can tell you if something’s right or wrong?”

My view on complexity has shifted. To address the two claims made in this section:

1. Previously, I thought perceiving the complexity of an opportunity when framing is important; now, I realize that framing is also an iterative process that requires simple steps. For our Praxis II RFP assignment ([link] to Praxis II), we attempted to address the complexity of the teamwork management opportunity by skipping verification and going straight to validation. We thought that only real-world validation could prove whether or not a design is good, since verification tests (such as measuring the loading time of the software) would be unable to capture what the clients actually want (to improve their team management). I now realize that even though people problems are complex, verification is still a valuable step -- without verification, we risk brining a half-finished design to users, which does not improve validation at all. We also tried to use complex, analog metrics (such as team velocity) instead of binary metrics (such as whether or not the design has a task tracking feature), which made quantifying performance harder. In all, trying to fit too much complexity into framing may hinder the process of design verification and validation; we should instead try to use simple verification metrics to build up to a more nuanced framing.

2. From the perspective of teamwork: I still believe that diverse viewpoints matter, and communicating with one's team members is great; however, I now believe that conciseness in communication is also very important. This view was formed by my Praxis II team members([link] to Praxis II). We had a great deal of fun working together, and we often had hour-long work sessions that seemed to pass at the blink of an eye. However, when I look back at what we did, I often find that we spent more time talking than actually producing written work. Although we communicated our diverse viewpoints well, we may have gotten the same work done in a shorter amount of time if we had been more concise with our communcation. If we generalize communication to a form of representation, we can infer that: there may be is a tradeoff between nuance (complexity) of representation and conciseness (simplicity) of representation; high-quality representation balances the two.

 

Summary: How I Aim to Embrace Complexity Based on My Personal Experience 

“Design is often too complicated for us to say what’s right and what’s wrong.” I leaned back on my chair. “I was also really uncomfortable with this at first. Surely, there must be a ‘best’ solution, right?”

“Yeah, of course. There are things that must be true – like, rocks will always sink in water, right?”

“But what if the water is frozen?”

“Big sister – you’re cheating!”

“I’m not. In design, what’s ‘better’ or ‘worse’ depends entirely on the context. That’s why it’s so complicated.”

I pointed to her math homework, laid out on the table. “Growing up in China, I’m so used to doing math questions with one definite answer. Only when I started engineering science did I realize how messy reality can get. No endpoint, no unique solutions – like you said, engineering design is hard work. But also, kind of fun.”

“I guess.” The girl sat upright, and closed her math exercise book. “Is that your position on engineering design, then?”

“Yeah. As much as possible, I’d like to move away from my student mindset (formed by my past experience), and move towards an engineering designer’s mindset (formed by praxis), which involves getting used to complexity and embracing it. I think – that’s my position statement.”

Position… statement…? 

The lights in MY150 suddenly started swimming in my vision, until they dimmed to pitch blackness. And then, I opened my eyes.

I woke up from my desk, my laptop in front of me. On the screen was a blank word document, my position statement waiting to be written.

I continue to believe that acknowledging nuance will help me becomes less of a student and more of an engineer; this view applies to my view on complexity as well. Before, I thought only about the benefits of complexity; now, I realized that overcomplicating things can bring issues -- my view on complexity has grew more nuanced, which makes me a better problem-solver and an better engineer.
