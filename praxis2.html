What My Process Taught Me About Complexity and Simplicity

Coding and Research

In our Praxis II design, I contributed to the firmware for the Raspberry Pi microcontroller; in the process, I learned that complex code is often built up from simple code; complex algorithms don't always perform better. I was working on extracting data from the drone's onboard storage, and there were two methods: either I go through drone storage to find the latest log to analyze, or I erase the drone storage every time after the diagnostic so only new logs are extracted. I initially tried the former approach, which didn't work, since I couldn't find the right logs. Continuining with the first approach, I spent days using agentic AI, trying to debug the code. I ended up with a extremely complicated algorithm, but it still didn't work. That when I asked about my approach on an online forum, where I found out that the first approach was actually impossible. I coded up the second approach, and it worked right away. Through this experience, I realized that a working solution may not necessarily be more complex than my original solution; when solutions become too complex, it may be time to go back to simpler approaches that were brought up in diverging.


Presentation

Through working on the Beta Release and Showcase poster, I realized that in a simpler representation is sometimes more effective. During the Beta Release, we tried to fill our trifold poster with slides, so that we could definintely cover all things on the rubric. However, this led our poster to become too cluttered -- assessors were unable to read our poster from a distance, and we had to speak very quickly to cover all slides on our poster. From this, I realized that even if the work we did was complex, overwhelming our representation of work with information is not a good idea. We had to learn to let go, and present only the information that was essential to get our point accross. I had to learn to trust that all the hard work we did will come accross, even if we could only represent "tip of the iceberg" of what we had worked on. For our showcase, we kept the advice we had in mind, and decided to focus the poster on a CAD rendering of our design. We did what we could to simplify our poster; however, it was still a bit information-dense compared to other groups' posters. From this, I realized that communicating concisely is sometime I need to work on.

As for writing style: through working with teammates, I realized that it's important to use precise technical terms, but complicated terminalogy isn't always equivalent to better communication. It's important to use the right terminologies to be precise and increase one's authority, but using too many technical terms will obscure meaning. This is what happened when I wrote reports with teammates -- due to my limited language abilities, I was unable to fully understand what my teammates wrote, which led me to be unable to edit their work well, causing me to ignore errors that I could have caught. On the other hand, teammates have pointed out that I have been too informal and unprecise with my writing. In the future, I will aim to find a good balance between using precise terminologies (complex lanugage) and clear language (simple language) when writing.




CTMFs Used

Metrics [Framing]

Previously, I thought metrics always had to be graduated, but now I realized that a simple binary metric can sometimes be more effective. For context: the RFP our team worked on worked on optimizing project management at a high school robotics team. Based on secondary research, we adopted agile metrics from online sources, since agile methodologies are often used in industrial project management. These metrics are graduated, but they related more to validation than verification, which led us to use unnecessarily-complicated metrics that did not suit the opportunity.
In our design project, we reframed the drone diagnostics RFP using simpler metrics. For example, we used a simple accuracy score to test our algorithm design, and pass/fail dimension check for portability. We also reduced unnecessary evaluation criteria. As a result, designing and performing tests for verification became more manageable.

Connection to complexity/simplicity: From my two constrasting experiences in Praxis II, I learned that graduated, complex metrics is not always a strength: although it might capture more aspects of the opportunity, it may also add effort without improving verification. Simpler metrics gave us clearer evidence and a more practical testing process.


Rescoping [Framing]

We scoped down the drone diagnostics RFP to achieve a more accomplishable design. Originally, the drone RFP's need was: "A reliable, diagnostic system to verify the integrity of the drone's systems, evaluate the flight-readiness of its core components, and prevent in-flight failure". When we dug into the research, we realized that there were many drone systems (propulsion system, electrical system, etc.); the broad scope would make the design space too unconstrained, making diverging difficult. As well, the goals are mapped from the broad design space, so it would be hard to find a design that satisfies all goals. Because of this, we decided to rescope, and only focus on the drone's propulsion system. Our rescoped need is as such: "A portable system to verify the health and flight readiness of drone propulsion systems using objective pre-flight diagnostics." Accordingly, we removed unnecessary goals relating to battery health. This narrowed scope allowed us to diverge more easily, facilitating our design process.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Rescoping reduced unnecessary complexity in our project. By narrowing from all drone systems to only the drone propulsion system, we made the design space constrained enough to diverge effectively. This simpler scope led to more manageable goals.


Functional Structural Diagram (FSD) [Representing]

Using functional structure diagrams (FSDs) to represent the drone's propulsion system helped us rescope and better diverge. After doing some preliminary research, we were flooded with information about drone fault detection methods, which led to us being confused about which input signals (such as noise or heat) would best represent drone status for our use case. Then, we learned about FSDs in lecture, and we tried drawing a FSD of the drone's propulsion system during Studio. This really helped us organize the unstructured research we did in a clear manner. By using a FSD to analyze the drone, we realized that we could map outputs of the drone propulsion system to various detection methods, which led to many design concepts. Therefore, having a graphical representation of the system inputs and outputs helped us structure our diverging.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Although FSD did simplify the huge amount of initial research that we did, being able to represent our research in a clear, graphical manner helped us with diverging. This is a case where concise representation initally seemed like a simplification and a constraint on the way we can think about our research, but it actually unlocked our creativity.
