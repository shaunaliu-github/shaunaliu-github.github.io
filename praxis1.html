Praxis 1 Design Summary

Design Opportunity - Acoustically Optimized Soda-Can Opening Device

Have you ever tried opening a soda can in a quiet lecture hall? Did the pop draw more attention than you wanted? This is the splartz (a minor inconvenience) we addressed with our design. We wanted to make a device or a modification to the soda can that could eliminate the loud pop. We wanted the design to benefit Engineering Science students, who were our primary stakeholders.

From stakeholder's statements, we framed our Need, Goals, and Objectives (NGOs). Our goals are as such:

  • Goal 1: The device shall reduce the peak noise created during can opening.
  • Goal 2: The device shall maintain the beverage's drinkability and integrity.
  • Goal 3: The device shall ensure high portability to facilitate student use.
  • Goal 4: The device shall enable frequent, repeated use while maintaining cleanability.
  • Goal 5: The device shall ensure accessibility for people with different physical abilities.
  • Goal 6: The device shall meet safety expectations for food contact and sharpness.

We then went through several rounds of diverging and converging, and converged on a final design.

Our recommended design was a soda can with a sliding tab. The gasket under the sliding tab would create a air-tight seal that traps the CO2 when the can is unopened. To open the can, the user would slide open the slider, which releases gas release slowly compared to exising designs, reducing the noise peak.

What This Project Taught Me About Complexity and Simplicity

In Praxis I, we had an oversimplified model for how the soda can worked, which led us to pivot our designs completely. We initially assumed that controlling depressurization would remove most of the opening noise, so we focused on reducing the gas hiss. We came up with design candidates that attempted to lengthen the pressure release time to reduce noise peak. However, when testing our design candidate prototypes, we were unable to reduce the noise. We then discovered that can opening noise also came from the aluminum tab tearing the can lid, not just from gas release. This caused us to widen the scope of our design, and include modifications to the soda can valid designs.

The failure of our early design candidates caused by oversimplifying the opportunity in our analysis. Our early depressurization model was a useful simplification, based on our assumptions of how a soda can worked, but it ignored the critical physical mechanism of the tearing metal tab. Prototyping testing exposed that hidden complexity and forced us to revise our assumptions. This connects directly to the Hoover Dam model: reality is complex, and every engineering model necessarily simplifies that complexity. Since we cannot avoid simplifications, we must test our design against reality through proxy testing or implementation.

CTMFs Used

Morph Chart [Diverging]

Morph chart were helpful to generate designs based on their functions. In a morph chart, functions of the design are listed as rows, and different ways to implement the function are added to the rows.

When coming up designs for the soda can opening device, morph charts helped us generate designs in a organized way by breaking down a complex function into simpler subfunctions. When we did classical brainstorming, we filled a page with design candidates, but the ideas were very unorganized, and some contained overlapping components. Using a Morph Charts helped us recognize the individuals subfunctions a design is supposed to accomplish and reduce overlap among components, which organized our diverging.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Morph Charts break down complex requirements into subfunctions, and break down complex design candidates into simpler components. It simplified the diverging process.

Proxy Testing [Converging] + Prototyping [Representing]

Proxy testing: Using a simpler prototpe/ metric to measure the performance of a more complicated model.

Prototyping: A representation of the final design, used to verify the design's effectiveness, and present the design to others.

It's often easier to proxy test and prototype specific parts of a design. To paraphrase from lecture: "Prototype only the unbelievable parts." When testing the soda can opener, the only "unbelievable part" was whether or not the design could effectively reduce the noise caused by opening the soda can. To do this, we made rough protypes whose functions was only to open the can; we did not worry about food safety or grip area while making these prototypes, even though they were also requirements. The prototypes were just functional enough for us to proxy test: we placed a calibrated phone microphone at 1 m (with 30 samples per second to align timestamps) and extracted peak amplitude relative to ambient baseline. This helped us evaluated the noise reduction effect of our design candidates.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: "Prototyping / Testing only the unbelievable parts" helped break down verification into simpler steps. Verification can be complex, since there are often many requirements and evaluation criteria; however, not every prototype / proxy test needs to address all the requirements and evaluation criteria all at once. We can use simple prototypes / proxy tests to build up to a complex claim.

Pugh Chart [Converging/Representing]

Pugh chart: A converging tool used to evaluate designs against verification metrics.

Pugh charts can help eliminate unreasonable designs quickly; however, it may be too simplified for later-stage converging. Our team used Pugh charts to bring down our pools of around 20 design candidates down to 4. We did this by ruling out designs that did not satisfy most of the requirements, or were impossible to prototype. However, when comparing the 4 remaining designs, we found measurement matrices more useful. Since we had protypes for each one of them, putting values in a measurement matrix gave us a sense of how good a design performs with respect to a specific metric, which gave us a more holistic way to evaluating a designs. Pugh charts can help us know at a glance how a design performs with a metric compared to the reference, but the "+" or "-" is not graduted. As a result, I found the measurement matrix to be more useful in our project.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Pugh charts create a clear representation of the "pros and cons" of each design, but it may be too simplified for later-stage converging. To quote from lecture: " Don't sum a Pugh Chart" -- this is because the "+" and "-" can be an oversimplification of how a design performs on a metric. The ultimate decisions, when converging, still belongs to the engineer, who is capable of holistically assessing designs and not overly relying on numbers.
