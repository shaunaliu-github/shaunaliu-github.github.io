Praxis 1 Design Summary

Design Opportunity - Acoustically Optimized Soda-Can Opening Device

Have you ever tried opening a soda can in a quiet lecture hall? Did the pop draw more attention than you wanted? This is the splartz (a minor inconvenience) we addressed with our design. We wanted to make a device or a modification to the soda can that could eliminate the loud pop. We wanted the design to benefit Engineering Science students, who were our primary stakeholders.

From stakeholder's statements, we framed our Need, Goals, and Objectives (NGOs). Our goals are as such:

  • Goal 1: The device shall reduce the peak noise created during can opening.
  • Goal 2: The device shall maintain the beverage's drinkability and integrity.
  • Goal 3: The device shall ensure high portability to facilitate student use.
  • Goal 4: The device shall enable frequent, repeated use while maintaining cleanability.
  • Goal 5: The device shall ensure accessibility for people with different physical abilities.
  • Goal 6: The device shall meet safety expectations for food contact and sharpness.

We then went through several rounds of diverging and converging, and converged on a final design.

Our recommended design was a soda can with a sliding tab. The gasket under the sliding tab would create a air-tight seal that traps the CO2 when the can is unopened. To open the can, the user would slide open the slider, which releases gas release slowly compared to exising designs, reducing the noise peak.

What These Projects Taught Me About Complexity and Simplicity

In Praxis I, prototyping changed how we converged on a "silent can" design. We initially assumed that controlling depressurization would remove most of the opening noise, so we focused on reducing the gas hiss. However, prototype testing showed that the dominant sound came from the aluminum tab tearing the can lid, not just from gas release.

This was a clear complexity and simplicity lesson. Our early model was a useful simplification, but it ignored a critical physical mechanism. The prototype exposed that hidden complexity and forced us to revise our assumptions, requirements, and design priorities using primary evidence rather than intuition.

This connects directly to the Hoover Dam model: reality is complex, and every engineering model simplifies that complexity. The goal is not to avoid simplification, but to test it against reality through implementation. Going forward, I will use prototyping earlier and more often to check whether my simplified models still capture the mechanisms that matter.
