Praxis 1 Design Summary

Design Opportunity - Acoustically Optimized Soda-Can Opening Device

Have you ever tried opening a soda can in a quiet lecture hall? Did the pop draw more attention than you wanted? This is the splartz (a minor inconvenience) we addressed with our design. We wanted to make a device or a modification to the soda can that could eliminate the loud pop. We wanted the design to benefit Engineering Science students, who were our primary stakeholders.

From stakeholders' statements, we framed our Need, Goals, and Objectives (NGOs). Our goals are:

  • Goal 1: The device shall reduce the peak noise created during can opening.
  • Goal 2: The device shall maintain the beverage's drinkability and integrity.
  • Goal 3: The device shall ensure high portability to facilitate student use.
  • Goal 4: The device shall enable frequent, repeated use while maintaining cleanability.
  • Goal 5: The device shall ensure accessibility for people with different physical abilities.
  • Goal 6: The device shall meet safety expectations for food contact and sharpness.

We then went through several rounds of diverging and converging, and converged on a final design.

Our recommended design was a soda can with a sliding tab. The gasket under the sliding tab would create an airtight seal that traps the CO2 when the can is unopened. To open the can, the user would slide open the slider, which releases gas more slowly than existing designs, reducing the noise peak.

What This Project Taught Me About Complexity and Simplicity

Confirmation Bias

By reflecting on evidence and collaborating with teammates, I recognized my bias for a certain prototype. In the first round of converging, I had a “favourite” design, which was to use a metal straw (with an extended handle for grip) to open the soda can, similar to how a nail can split open wood. If we only considered the screw-on mechanism, the longer lever arm of the extended handle would theoretically greatly reduce the force needed to open the can. However, we were unable to make a metal prototype with a tip that would be sharp enough to split open aluminum. I regarded this as a limitation in prototyping, but my teammates brought up that if the metal straw was sharp enough to split aluminum, it would be sharp enough to hurt people. After several back-and-forth discussions, I realized that I was only defending the metal straw idea because it was one of the first designs we came up with. Upon carefully considering the testing evidence and my teammates' arguments, I changed my idea. I learned through this experience that I am sometimes influenced by confirmation bias; however, reflecting critically about the evidence and discussing with teammates helped me overcome my bias.

Biases tend to lead us to simplify the complexity of the opportunity; considering different (complex) opinions helps us correct this bias. When modelling the opportunity, we always have to simplify; what we leave out in our simplification becomes our bias. However, different people may perceive the situation differently, leading to different simplifications. If we take advantage of this fact and listen to others (who are "little a" authorities in the Perry Model) with open-mindedness, we may be able to have a more holistic (complex) model of reality that contains fewer biases. So, in teamwork, we can reach complexity by considering evidence critically, and by listening to others.

Oversimplified Model

In our early stage design, we had an oversimplified model for how the soda can worked, which led us to pivot our designs completely. We initially assumed that controlling depressurization would remove most of the opening noise, so we focused on reducing the gas hiss. We came up with design candidates that attempted to lengthen the pressure release time to reduce noise peak. However, when testing our design candidate prototypes, we were unable to reduce the noise. We then discovered that can opening noise also came from the aluminum tab tearing the can lid, not just from gas release. This caused us to widen the scope of our design, and include modifications to the soda can as valid designs.

The failure of our early design candidates was caused by oversimplifying the opportunity in our analysis. Our early depressurization model was a useful simplification, based on our assumptions of how a soda can worked, but it ignored the critical physical mechanism of the tearing metal tab. Prototype testing exposed that hidden complexity and forced us to revise our assumptions. This connects directly to the Hoover Dam model: reality is complex, and every engineering model necessarily simplifies that complexity. Since we cannot avoid simplifications, we must test our design against reality through proxy testing or implementation.

CTMFs Used

Morph Chart [Diverging]

Morph chart: A diverging tool that lists different implementations of sub-functions of a design, which are combined to make new designs. Morph charts were helpful for generating designs based on their functions.

When coming up with designs for the soda-can opening device, morph charts helped us generate designs in an organized way by breaking down a complex function into simpler subfunctions. When we did classical brainstorming, we filled a page with design candidates, but the ideas were very unorganized, and some contained overlapping components. Using morph charts helped us recognize the individual subfunctions a design is supposed to accomplish and reduce overlap among components, which organized our diverging.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Morph Charts break down complex requirements into subfunctions, and break down complex design candidates into simpler components. It simplified the diverging process.

Proxy Testing [Converging] + Prototyping [Representing]

Proxy testing: Using a simpler prototype/ metric to measure the performance of a more complicated model.

Prototyping: A representation of the final design, used to verify the design's effectiveness, and present the design to others.

It's often easier to proxy test and prototype specific parts of a design. To paraphrase from lecture: "Prototype only the unbelievable parts." When testing the soda can opener, the only "unbelievable part" was whether or not the design could effectively reduce the noise caused by opening the soda can. To do this, we made rough prototypes whose function was only to open the can; we did not worry about food safety or grip area while making these prototypes, even though they were also requirements. The prototypes were just functional enough for us to proxy test: we placed a calibrated phone microphone at 1 m (with 30 samples per second to align timestamps) and extracted peak amplitude relative to ambient baseline. This helped us evaluate the noise reduction effect of our design candidates.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: "Prototyping / Testing only the unbelievable parts" helped break down verification into simpler steps. Verification can be complex, since there are often many requirements and evaluation criteria; however, not every prototype / proxy test needs to address all the requirements and evaluation criteria all at once. We can use simple prototypes / proxy tests to build up to a complex claim.

Pugh Chart [Converging/Representing]

Pugh chart: A converging tool used to evaluate designs against verification metrics.

Pugh charts can help eliminate unreasonable designs quickly; however, they may be too simplified for later-stage converging. Our team used Pugh charts to bring our pool of around 20 design candidates down to 4. We did this by ruling out designs that did not satisfy most of the requirements, or were impossible to prototype. However, when comparing the 4 remaining designs, we found measurement matrices more useful. Since we had prototypes for each one of them, putting values in a measurement matrix gave us a sense of how good a design performs with respect to a specific metric, which gave us a more holistic way to evaluate designs. Pugh charts can help us know at a glance how a design performs with a metric compared to the reference, but the "+" or "-" is not graduated. As a result, I found the measurement matrix to be more useful in our project.

Connected to complexity/simplicity: Pugh charts create a clear representation of the "pros and cons" of each design, but they may be too simplified for later-stage converging. To quote from lecture: " Don't sum a Pugh Chart" -- this is because the "+" and "-" can be an oversimplification of how a design performs on a metric. The ultimate decision, when converging, still belongs to the engineer, who is capable of holistically assessing designs and not overly relying on numbers.
